# **WHY I QUIT EDITING**

## **Hook**

Why does getting better at editing not actually make you more money?

For two years, I did everything you're supposed to do. Pirated all the plugins. Mastered After Effects. Watched every tutorial...

And you know what happened? My income barely moved. I was scraping by, making $1K on a good month, stressing about where the next video would come from.

Then I stopped focusing on editing. Half a year later: $8K a month from one client.

Same software. Same technical skills. What changed was how I thought about my role entirely.

Most editors are button-pushers. They wait for instructions, execute, and send the video back. But there's a ceiling on what button-pushers can charge.

But the editors making real money? They've made a shift that most don't even know exists.

---

## **Chapter 1: The Button-Pusher**

I met Marco in July, 2023\. After two years of grinding, I'd landed a client that felt like a real opportunity, and Marco was the creative director for that client; he was doing all the strategy and managing me alongside a few other editors.

I was ready to overdeliver, so I would spend an hour micro-tweaking the easing of the keyframes in a 5-second animation. Back then, that was my idea of "good editing": are there enough animations, and do they look smooth enough?

But the revisions Marco would give me? Never about any of that. Never about my keyframes or transitions. It was about completely different things. And so I couldn't even tell if my work was any good.

See, Marco wasn’t a good editor mechanically. His After Effects skills were nothing special. But the videos he produced? You'd watch them in one sitting. Couldn't look away. You wouldn't even notice the editing because you were so sucked into the story.

And one thing was clear:

He was evaluating videos by something entirely different. And at the time, I didn't even know what that was.

---

## **Chapter 2: What, Not How**

When I was a button-pusher, I had one fear that controlled every edit I made.

If I didn't have an animation for 30 seconds, I would start to panic and try to force an animation there, no matter what.

I was so scared the client wouldn't accept the video if I hadn’t filled every second with movement. And so I brought this up on one of the calls with Marco:

I said, "We didn’t have any animations for the past minute here. Should I add something?"

Marco didn't even hesitate. "Nah, that's totally fine. The guy’s just telling a story. The viewer is interested in what he's saying. It doesn't need any visuals."

That was the first time something clicked. Marco wasn't looking at the video from the editor’s perspective. He was thinking about the viewer.

Most editors ask: HOW do I make this look good? How do I animate this?

But the ones who made the creative director shift and are actually editing great videos?

They ask: WHAT should the viewer see right now? What do they need to understand? What would make them feel like the video is delivering on its promise?

The "what" comes first. The "how" is just execution.

And the thing is, Marco tried teaching this to other editors. They wouldn't listen.

They kept forcing their own style onto the videos. They'd add animations for phrases that the viewer didn't even care about. The phrases that they should have removed entirely. Instead, they added flashy effects on top to try to make it look cool.

The truth is: the viewer doesn't care about that stuff.

Worrying about HOW to show is what keeps you stuck under $2k/month and makes you waste hours on unnecessary animations. Knowing WHAT to show and WHAT not to show... that's the creative director skill set you should be after.

When I made this shift, everything changed. I stopped asking "how do I make this intro flashy?" and started asking "the viewer just clicked on this title and thumbnail. That's all they know. What do they expect to see next?"

It sounds simple. But it changes everything about how you edit.

## **Chapter 3: When Less Is More**

My favourite quote ever is from Steve Jobs. He said "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication."

If you imagine the midwit curve meme, where the guy on the left and the guy on the right end up doing the same thing, while the guy in the middle is trying too hard. That's exactly what's happening with editors.

You start simple because you don’t know any better. Then you go through the button-pusher phase of learning all the tools, techniques, plugins, where everything has to be complex. Flashy. Over-produced.

And this is the make-or-break point. You either get stuck here forever, or come out the other side and land back on simple. But it's a different kind of simple. It's intentional simple, way better than simple simple, because it tells the viewer everything they need to know without overwhelming them.

That's why it's the ULTIMATE sophistication.

Marco understood this before I did. He would leave a static, a bit ugly graphic on screen for 5, 10, sometimes 15 seconds. No animation. No movement. Just sitting there.

In a normal editor world, that's a big no-no. You want things moving. You want stuff popping up. You want the video to feel dynamic.

But Marco understood something most editors don't: if the graphic is showing something valuable to the viewer, they'd want to look at it and study it for longer, they'd need TIME to actually process it.

If he had added crazy keyframes and flashy animations, the video would have been worse. The viewer would have gotten distracted by the editing instead of actually absorbing the information.

I can't tell you how many times I've created a complex animation, then intentionally stripped it back. Removed elements. Simplified the movement. Not because I couldn't make it complex. Because the viewer didn't need the complexity. They needed the point.

That's the creative director skill set. Going through the complexity and coming out the other side with something simple.

## **Chapter 4: What Simple Looks Like**

Every single video I edited in the last 3 years has this one type of graphic no one talks about.

I call it the Blockout Graphic.

It's one graphic that you show multiple times throughout the video. You reveal or change elements as the video progresses. The viewer sees the same structure again and again, but each time something is different.

It doesn't look impressive in a portfolio. Other editors would probably think it's boring.

But here's why it works.

Most editors think the number one thing that makes viewers click off is boredom. That's why they're scared to show still graphics. That's why they add movement to everything.

Wrong.

The number one thing that makes viewers click off is confusion.

Think about a 15-minute video. That's a lot of information. If you don't structure it, it feels like one long sentence. The viewer loses track of where they are. They get confused. They leave.

The Blockout Graphic solves this.

I break every video down into what I like to call Story Blocks. Distinct sections with clear purposes. Then I have all the Story Blocks in my Blockout Graphic and always keep the viewer grounded and in the story by showing which Story Block we’re in.

And more often than not, I even have Blockout Graphics inside Story Blocks.

Every time I pop up the same graphic with one element changed, I'm telling a visual story. The viewer sees progression. They know exactly where they are. They never get lost.

I apply the same principle to all other visuals:

What does the viewer need to see to understand the story of the video?

Creator talks about a topic rising in popularity? Pull up the actual Google Trends graph, instead of creating your own flashy visualization with deep glow and trim paths.

Creator mentions a salary from a job listing? Show the actual LinkedIn article.

Creator references another creator? Show their real profile picture, not a generic avatar.

These aren't complex animations. They're the actual thing. The viewer doesn't need you to create a fancy visualization. They need to see what's being talked about and never feel lost.

My editing works because I think about WHAT to show, not HOW to make it flashy.

## **Chapter 5: Think More, Execute Less**

Here's what actually changed when I went from $1K to $8K a month in less than a year.

I didn't get better at After Effects. I didn't learn new plugins; in fact, I stopped using all of them, and now my renders take 10 times less time, and I haven’t gotten faster at executing.

Instead, I started spending way more time thinking. And way less time executing.

Let me break this down:

Decide WHAT before HOW. That's thinking.

Decide IF you need the animation before creating it. That's thinking.

When you do add, start complex and strip it down to essentials. That's thinking.

Plan your blockout graphic before making it. That's thinking.

Find the actual thing instead of creating a visualization. That's thinking.

Button-pushers spend hours creating pointless graphics. They add animations that should never exist. They build complexity that confuses viewers. Then they get revisions. And redo half of it.

Creative directors think before they execute. They only create what needs to exist. They're not perfecting redundant animations. They're intentional about their editing, and they do it for one purpose and this purpose only:

To make the video better for the viewer.

The result? They’re faster. And the video is better.

That's the shift. Creative Director doesn't mean "better executor." It means "better thinker."
