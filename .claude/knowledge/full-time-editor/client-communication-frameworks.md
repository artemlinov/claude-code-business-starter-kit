# CLIENT COMMUNICATION FRAMEWORKS

Complete reference for teaching video editors how to communicate with clients. Covers closing inbound clients, raising prices, handling tricky situations, and negotiation principles.

Refined through real coaching sessions with FTE students (Alejo, David, Dinal, Dmitry, Tiago, Jarus, Lucca) and tested through mock conversations.

---

# Section 1: Core Philosophy

## Communication IS Sales

Every message an editor sends is either building trust and positioning them as a strategic partner, or commoditizing them as an order-taker. Most editors fail before they ever start editing because they communicate like vendors, not partners.

## You're Not Selling Editing

You're selling the transformation from their current content situation to their dream content situation. The price isn't about "how much does editing cost." It's about "what's it worth to close this gap between where they are and where they want to be?"

## Tailored, Not Templated

People want to feel like special snowflakes. When they spend money, they want something tailored specifically for them, not just something you do for other people. This is why research matters, why understanding their specific situation matters, and why generic templates don't work.

## The Tagline

"If you say your price before you understand their problem, you're just another freelancer on Upwork."

# Section 2: Closing Inbound Clients (Price Last™ Framework)

## The 2-Phase Flow

The closing process happens across two environments, each with different rules.

### Phase 1: Social Platform (X, Instagram, etc.)

**Goal:** Show you did research, align on logistics, get off the platform. That's it.

**Rules:**
- DMs must be SHORT and casual. This is a text conversation, not a business proposal.
- Every extra message on social platforms drastically increases the risk of getting ghosted. Move fast.
- Don't ask about vision, style, or pain on social platforms. Just logistics.
- Don't assume what's wrong with their content. No "your vlogs have a lot of room to elevate pacing." That's cocky and you don't know yet.
- Don't ask for things you can look up yourself. If their channel is public, go look at it.

**The Steps:**

1. **Research first.** Before sending any message, look at their channel. Note video format, average length, upload frequency. Never ask the client basic questions you can answer by spending 2 minutes on their YouTube.

2. **First message: show research + ask about volume.** Short and casual. "Yeah, I have some availability right now. I see you're making 10-15 minute talking head videos. That's exactly what I specialize in. How many videos per month are you looking to make?"

3. **Check if it's a fit.** If the client wants 4 videos/month but the editor can only do 2, nothing else matters. Volume alignment comes before everything.

4. **Funnel to personal platform immediately.** As soon as volume aligns: "Perfect, I can deliver [X] for you consistently. Would WhatsApp or Telegram work better? X DMs are a bit hectic." Get off social platforms as fast as possible.

### Phase 2: Personal Platform (WhatsApp/Telegram)

**Goal:** Get to price in 3-4 messages. Don't beat around the bush.

**The Steps:**

5. **Confirm logistics + ask about style.** "So you're looking for 4 talking head videos per month. My price is gonna depend on the style you're looking for. Are you happy with what you currently have, or are you looking to switch things up?"

   Important framing: "My price is gonna depend on the style" is natural. "Before I give you a number" sounds salesy. Small wording differences matter.

6. **Drop the price.** Use the client's exact numbers back to them: "For 4 talking head videos per month at 10 to 15 minutes each, with a step up in polish, my rate would be $X per video. Let me know if this fits your budget." Don't over-justify. Don't explain what the price "allows you to do." Confident price + simple close.

**Key principles for the pricing message:**
- Use exact numbers from the conversation (not "at that length" but "at 10 to 15 minutes each")
- Only reference what the client actually said (don't invent "tighter overall finish" if they never said that)
- Keep the close simple ("let me know if this fits your budget")
- If multiple formats, suggest different pricing for each since they're different workloads
- Connect the price to the client's specific need (consistency, quality upgrade, or removing management burden)

### Why Logistics Before Vision/Pain

The original framework went: research → vision → pain → bridge → price. This was refined because:

1. If volume doesn't match availability, the rest doesn't matter
2. Asking about vision and pain on social platforms creates too many messages (ghosting risk)
3. Pain often comes up naturally without fishing for it
4. Getting to price efficiently shows professionalism, not desperation

The refined flow is: research → volume → funnel → style → price. Tighter, more efficient, lower ghosting risk.

### If Pain Points Come Up Naturally

Don't go fishing with multiple rounds of pain-discovery questions. But if the client volunteers pain ("my current editor keeps missing deadlines"), acknowledge it briefly and use it when connecting price to their need.

The biggest pain points for creators:
- Editors who miss deadlines
- Editors who don't pay attention to details
- Editors who never learn from revisions
- Editors who do all of the above AND miss deadlines

If the client brings these up, use them. If they don't, just move to price based on volume, format, and style.

# Section 3: Handling Price Pushback

This is where most editors panic. The masterclass should cover this explicitly because it happens in almost every negotiation.

## The "Consistent Work" Reframe

When a client says "can you go down since it's consistent work?" they're framing consistency as a favor to the editor. The truth: consistency benefits the CLIENT more. They get reliability, predictable output, growth. The editor isn't getting a favor.

## Two Paths

**Holding firm:**
- Anchor the original rate to the value/outcome
- Offer an alternative that protects the rate: reduce scope/volume instead of price
- "If [lower budget] works better for you, we could do [fewer videos] at the current rate so the quality stays high"
- This reframes from "is the editor worth it?" to "how much can the client invest?"

**Adjusting slightly:**
- Make the lower rate CONDITIONAL on commitment ("if we lock in 4 per month consistently")
- Don't just drop the price because they asked. Trade commitment for price
- Keep the adjustment slight, not dramatic
- Signal that the original rate is valid. The discount is earned through commitment, not because the price was wrong

## The Psychological Principle

When the editor offers a scope reduction instead of a price drop, two things happen:
1. It shows they're not desperate
2. It reframes the decision as scope vs. price, not "is this editor worth it?"

When the editor offers a conditional discount, it protects their value while still giving the client a win. Both parties feel good.

# Section 4: Raising Prices with Existing Clients

Seven patterns, each for a different situation. The editor picks the one that fits.

## Pattern A: The Life Change Frame

**When:** Editor has a genuine life change (going full-time, starting school, moving).

**Structure:** Appreciate the relationship → share the life change honestly → show excitement about THEIR goals → state the new rate → reinforce what improves.

**Why it works:** The client isn't hearing "I want more money." They're hearing "my life is changing and here's how I'm adapting." Pairing honesty about your situation with genuine excitement about their goals turns an awkward conversation into a moment of connection.

**Teaching insight from Alejo's case:** Alejo was nervous about raising from $155 to $300+ with his brother-in-law. The key was framing around his commitment to going full-time (honest life change), excitement about the client's goals of hitting 30K subscribers, AND adding new value (uploading, scheduling, eliminating typos). The raise wasn't just "more money" — it was a new chapter for both of them.

## Pattern B: The Value-Add Raise

**When:** Editor can genuinely offer additional value alongside the raise.

**Structure:** Appreciate relationship + shared goals → propose new rate → introduce new deliverables → mention improvement commitments → close with enthusiasm.

**Why it works:** The client isn't paying more for the same thing. They're getting an upgrade. The raise feels like a natural evolution, not a penalty.

**Teaching insight from Alejo's case:** Alejo noticed his client's YouTube descriptions were basically the same from video to video. Offering to handle uploads and scheduling was a real value-add. After 2-3 videos, the client wouldn't even need to open YouTube Studio. That's time and mental energy returned to the client.

## Pattern C: The Options Technique

**When:** Editor wants to give the client agency instead of an ultimatum. Especially powerful when genuinely struggling with workload.

**Structure:** Be honest about the situation → acknowledge recent issues → present two options (reduce volume OR increase rate) → let them choose.

**Why it works:** Instead of "pay me more or I leave," it's "here are two paths, both work for me." The client feels respected and in control. Both options should be real — the editor should genuinely be okay with either outcome.

**Teaching insight from David's case:** David had been missing deadlines and forgetting to upload podcasts to Spotify after starting uni. Instead of hiding this, the message owned it: "I know I've been slipping, and here's why." The two options — pull back to 2 episodes/month OR go to $750/episode — gave the client control. Naming the problem before the client had to showed self-awareness.

## Pattern D: The New Opportunity Leverage

**When:** Editor has a legitimate new client opportunity affecting bandwidth.

**Structure:** Inform (don't threaten) → explain the situation → state the consequence honestly → remove pressure: "No urgent action items. Just letting you know."

**Why it works:** This is NOT an ultimatum. It's transparency. "No urgent action items — just letting you know" removes all pressure. The client processes and responds on their terms.

**Teaching insight from Dinal's case:** Dinal had a new client opportunity. The advice was to frame it exactly as information-sharing: "It just so happened that today I got in contact with another client. If I start working with that guy, I wouldn't have enough time for your videos." No countdown timer. No "respond by Friday." Just honest communication.

## Pattern E: The Accountability + Raise

**When:** Editor has had performance issues AND wants to raise prices simultaneously.

**Structure:** Own mistakes directly → explain honestly → apologize → frame the raise as the solution: "For me to prioritize your work, I'd need [new rate]."

**Why it works:** Counter-intuitive, but owning mistakes before asking for more money builds trust. The editor is saying: "I couldn't deliver what you deserve at the current rate, and here's the proof — I've been slipping." The raise becomes the fix, not the ask.

**Teaching insight from David's case:** David's missed deadlines and Spotify upload were the honest evidence. The raise to $750 was positioned as: "this is what I need to prioritize your content over everything else." The client hears: "if I pay more, I get the version of this editor who doesn't miss deadlines."

## Pattern F: The Positive Momentum Raise

**When:** Client just praised the editor's work or there's genuine positive energy.

**Structure:** Express enthusiasm → short transition ("I'd love to keep working long-term") → one honest constraint → state new rate → keep it SHORT.

**Why it works:** Timing matters. Right after "this video was fire" is when value is most obvious. The message should be short because the evidence is fresh. Over-explaining undermines confidence.

**Teaching insight from Tiago's case:** The agency said "the video was fire." This was the perfect moment. Additionally, Tiago couldn't freely post his work (agency restrictions), which limited his growth. This restriction is legitimate leverage for a higher rate — it's a real business cost the editor absorbs.

## Pattern G: The Restructure

**When:** Editor wants to fundamentally redesign the entire working relationship.

**Structure:** Be honest about what's not working → show understanding of client's goals → propose a completely new structure (responsibilities, workflow, deliverables, pricing) → frame as "a better way to work together" → share personal goals honestly.

**Why it works:** Sometimes a straight price raise isn't right. The entire collaboration needs redesigning. When an editor proposes a new structure, they're showing strategic thinking. They're evolving from editor into production partner.

**Teaching insight from Jarus's case:** Jarus was spending 1-2 months on videos meant to take 1-2 weeks due to extreme perfectionism. Instead of hiding this, the message owned it and proposed a new approach: take over full production (scripts, voiceovers, editing) with an MVP mindset, deliver weekly instead of monthly. The client hears a vision for how this could actually work better.

# Section 5: Tricky Client Situations

## Responsibility Separation

**When:** Client tries to pay less because a video didn't perform, or blames the editor for things outside their control.

**Principle:** The editor's responsibility is execution, quality, and deadlines. The client's responsibility is the creative vision — the idea, topic, strategy. If the client decided to make a video knowing the idea might not perform, and the editor executed well, full payment. The editor gets paid the same regardless of performance. The client takes the W when a video blows up and takes the L when it doesn't.

**Teaching insight from Dmitry's case:** Dmitry's client wanted to pay $300 instead of $600 because he thought the video wouldn't perform. But the client HIMSELF admitted "this format isn't performing that well anymore" and "viewers are familiar with my story." He decided to make the video anyway — then wanted Dmitry to absorb the risk of his bad strategic call. The advice: push for $500 minimum. If they don't want to pay full price, suggest scrapping the video entirely. Result: Dmitry got $525.

## The Alternative Frame

**When:** Client proposes something unfair.

**Principle:** Offer an alternative that makes the unfair option less appealing. "If you don't feel this video is worth making at full price, maybe we should scrap it entirely." The client either pays fairly or loses the work. Deliver without aggression — practical suggestion, not a threat.

## Owning Mistakes

**When:** Editor genuinely messed up.

**Principle:** Own it directly → brief explanation → genuine apology → what changes going forward → concrete solution if relevant. Clients respect editors who own mistakes because most freelancers make excuses or ghost. Direct ownership + clear solution turns a negative into trust.

# Section 6: Message Writing Principles

These apply to every client message, regardless of situation.

## Core Principles

1. **Lead with client benefit.** The client should immediately see what they get. They are the important person.

2. **One clear idea per message.** If it doesn't support the main point, cut it.

3. **Stay human.** Vulnerable, honest, no corporate BS. Should sound like a real person, not a bot.

4. **Don't overwhelm.** One question at a time. Multiple questions = overwhelming.

5. **Writing process:** Brain dump everything → refine (make it about the client) → shorten (cut fluff).

6. **Right framing.** NEVER position yourself as the big shot doing the client a favor. "I'm super busy but I MIGHT squeeze you in" = wrong. You're there to serve their vision.

7. **Confirm + deadline.** When receiving work or direction: "Got everything. All clear. I'm on it. Will be done by [day]."

8. **Never lie.** There's always an honest framing that works.

9. **Educated guesses over basic questions.** Don't ask what you can figure out by looking at their channel.

## Platform-Specific Rules

**Social platforms (X, Instagram DMs):**
- SHORT and casual. These are texts, not emails.
- Every extra message = higher ghosting risk
- Get logistics aligned, then move to personal platform ASAP
- Don't have deep conversations here

**Personal platforms (WhatsApp, Telegram):**
- More room for detail, but still concise
- Get to the point efficiently
- 3-4 messages max before price when closing inbound
- This is where the real conversation happens

**Email:**
- More formal, but still human
- One clear idea per email
- Lead with what they need to know

## What Makes Messages Sound AI-Generated (Avoid)

- Long em dashes (—)
- Corporate speak or overly formal language
- Excessive politeness padding
- Generic phrases that could apply to anyone
- Perfectly structured paragraphs with topic sentences
- Over-justifying after stating a price

## The Research-First Principle

Never ask clients questions you can answer yourself. Looking at their YouTube channel for 2 minutes tells you format, length, frequency, and style. Making educated guesses based on research separates you from every editor who just asks "what's your budget?"

**Wrong:** "How long are your videos? How often do you post?"
**Right:** "I see you're making 10-15 minute talking heads. Are you looking to keep that going or ramp up?"

## Don't Assume Pain

Never make assumptions about what's wrong with the client's content. "Your vlogs have a lot of room to elevate pacing and storytelling" is cocky. You don't know what their pain is until they tell you. Reference only what the client has explicitly said.

# Section 7: Non-Negotiable Communication Rules

1. **Never lie.** Self-explanatory.

2. **Never ghost.** Always respond. Always follow up. Radio silence kills trust.

3. **Always confirm you're on it.** When receiving footage/direction: confirm everything is clear, say "I'm on it," and give a deadline. Clients hire faster when they know when they'll get their work.

4. **Move to personal platforms.** Earn it first (do research, provide initial value), then suggest WhatsApp or Telegram. Less noise, no ghosting, more serious conversation.

5. **Don't set hard revision limits.** Instead, communicate your commitment to learning their style so revisions disappear over time.

# Section 8: Key Teaching Insights

These are the principles that emerged from coaching real students through real negotiations. These are the "aha moments" worth teaching.

1. **Logistics before vision.** If volume doesn't match availability, nothing else matters. Check this first.

2. **Every extra message on social = ghosting risk.** Students need to understand that X and Instagram DMs are fragile. Get off the platform fast.

3. **"Before I give you a number" sounds salesy.** "My price depends on the style you're looking for" is natural. Small wording differences matter.

4. **Use the client's exact numbers.** "For 4 talking heads at 10-15 minutes each" hits differently than "at that length for that volume." Specificity makes the price feel tailored.

5. **Don't over-justify the price.** A confident price + "let me know if this fits your budget" is stronger than three sentences explaining what the price "allows you to do." Over-justifying signals insecurity.

6. **Consistency benefits the client more than the editor.** When clients ask for a "volume discount," they're framing consistency as a favor. It's not. The client gets reliability and growth. Reframe accordingly.

7. **Trade commitment for price, never just drop it.** If you adjust, make it conditional: "if we lock in X per month consistently." The original rate is valid. The discount is earned, not given.

8. **Hold firm by restructuring scope.** Instead of lowering price, offer fewer videos at the same rate. This reframes the decision from "is the editor worth it?" to "how many videos can I invest in?"

9. **Own mistakes before asking for more.** Counter-intuitive but powerful. When you've been slipping and want to raise prices, acknowledge the slipping first. The raise becomes the solution, not the ask.

10. **The GPT exists as a real-time tool.** Students can use the FTE Negotiator GPT during actual negotiations. The masterclass teaches the thinking; the GPT helps with execution in the moment.
