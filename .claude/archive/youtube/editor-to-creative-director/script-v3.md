# WHY I QUIT EDITING

## Hook

Why does getting better at editing not actually make you more money?

For two years, I did everything you're supposed to do. Pirated all the plugins. Mastered After Effects. Watched every tutorial...

And you know what happened? My income barely moved. I was scraping by, making $1K on a good month, stressing about where the next video would come from.

Then I stopped focusing on editing. Half a year later: $8K a month from one client.

Same software. Same technical skills. What changed was how I thought about my role entirely.

Most editors are button-pushers. They wait for instructions, execute, send the video back. And there's a ceiling on what button-pushers can charge.

The editors making real money? They've made a shift that most editors don't even know exists.

---

## Chapter 1: What, Not How

I met Marco in July, 2023. And he was not a good editor.

Technically, he was kind of bad. His After Effects skills were nothing special. He wasn't doing anything flashy.

But the videos he produced? You'd watch them in one sitting. Couldn't look away. You wouldn't even notice the editing because you were so sucked into the story.

Marco was a producer and he was basically managing me and a few other editors, so we were working together. And one thing about him stood out immediately.

He wasn't thinking about how to make the video look cool. He was thinking about what the viewer wanted to see.

He would leave a static, a bit ugly graphic on screen for 5, 10, sometimes 15 seconds. No animation. No movement. Just sitting there.

In a normal editor world, that's a big no-no. You want things moving. You want stuff popping up. You want the video to feel dynamic.

But Marco understood something most editors don't: if the graphic is showing something valuable to the viewer, they'd want to look at it and study it for longer, they'd need TIME to actually process it.

That's the framework he taught me, even if he never said it out loud.

Most editors ask: HOW do I make this look good? How do I animate this? How do I make this transition smoother?

But the ones who made the creative director shift and now are actually editing great video?

They ask: WHAT should the viewer see right now? What do they need to understand? What would make them feel like this video is delivering on its promise?

The "what" comes first. The "how" is just execution.

## Chapter 2: The Fear That Keeps You Stuck

When I was a button-pusher, I had one fear that controlled every edit I made.

If I didn'e have an animation for 30 seconds? I'd panic.
45 seconds? I'd spend hours trying to come up with an idea for an animation to add.
A full minute of just A-roll? No way. I thought I was definitely gonna get 50 revisions on that.

That fear is what keeps editors stuck.

You're so scared the client won't accept the video that you fill every second with movement. But you're solving the wrong problem.

The client doesn't care if there's an animation every 10 seconds. The client cares if the video works. If viewers understand the content. If the video delivers on its promise.

When I made the shift from an editor to a creative director that fear disappeared.

Now I had a reason behind every decision. If there wasn't an animation for 45 seconds, it was because just listening to what's being said was exactly what the viewer wanted.

But on the flip side, there are segments of the video that need visuals for them to make sense. That's where I'd add the animations, because they serve the viewer and the story.



My favourite quote ever is from Steve Jobs. He said "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication."

If you imagine the midwit curve meme, where the guy on the left and the guy on the right end up doing the same thing, while the guy in the middle is trying too hard.

That's exactly what's happening with editors. They try too hard to show off their skills so that the client feels like the video is worth their money.

But more keyframes and animations doesn't mean a better video. Knowing what to add AND what to remove... that's the creative director skill set you should be after.

    And the thing is, Marco tried teaching this to other editors. They wouldn't listen.

    They kept forcing their own style onto the videos. They'd add animations for phrases that the viewer didn't even care about.

    The phrases that they should have removed entirely. Instead, they added flashy effects on top to make it look cool.

    Adding things isn't creative direction. Knowing what to add AND what to remove... that's creative direction.

When I made this shift, everything changed. I stopped asking "how do I make this intro flashy?" and started asking "the viewer just clicked on this title and thumbnail. That's all they know. What do they expect to see next?"

It sounds simple. But it changes everything about how you edit.

But now, you might be thinking: "All this thinking on top of executing is going to take way more time."

Here's what actually happens.

Button-pushers spend hours creating graphics that end up getting cut. They add flair to redundant phrases. They build things that don't serve the viewer. Then they get revisions. And redo half of it.

When you think before you execute, you only create things that need to exist.

You're faster. And the video is better.

## Chapter 3: The Graphic That Made Me

Every single video I edited in the last 3 years has this one type of graphic no one talks about.

I call it the Blockout Graphic.

It's one graphic that you show multiple times throughout the video. You reveal or change elements as the video progresses. The viewer sees the same structure again and again, but each time something is different.

It doesn't look impressive in a portfolio. Other editors would probably think it's boring.

But here's why it works.

Most editors think the number one thing that makes viewers click off is boredom. That's why they're scared to show still graphics. That's why they add movement to everything.

Wrong.

The number one thing that makes viewers click off is confusion.

Think about a 15-minute video. That's a lot of information. If you don't structure it, it feels like one long sentence. The viewer loses track of where they are. They don't know what section they're in. They don't know how the example connects to the main point.

They get confused. They leave.

The Blockout Graphic solves this.

I break every video into what I call Story Blocks. Distinct sections with clear purposes. And within each Story Block, I use a Blockout Graphic to guide the viewer through.

Every time I pop up the same graphic with one element changed, I'm telling a visual story. The viewer sees progression. They know exactly where they are. They never get lost.

Here's a real example.

I was editing an interview where the founder talked about building two different apps. The host asked: "How did you come up with the idea for both of these?"

Without the Blockout Graphic, his answer would feel random. Just a guy talking about two separate things.

With the Blockout Graphic, I structured both explanations the same way. Same graphic format. Same progression. Suddenly his answer felt like a repeatable blueprint. Something the viewer could actually copy.

That's what makes this graphic powerful. It doesn't just display information. It creates structure. It turns random answers into frameworks.

## Chapter 4: Show, Don't Tell

Abody is one of the editors in my community. He was editing a video about what to do if your YouTube video is dying after 24 hours.

In the intro, he showed a view count: 400 views.

Seems reasonable. Video about dying videos. 400 views looks like a dying video.

But here's the problem.

The people watching aren't all small creators. Some have decent-sized channels. For them, even a dying video gets more than 400 views.

When they see 400 on screen, they think: "This isn't for me. My videos get more than that even when they flop."

Abody just lost a chunk of his audience in the first 10 seconds.

Now look at the thumbnail. It shows a YouTube analytics graph plummeting after 24 hours. That downward curve.

That's what the viewer clicked for. They saw that graph and thought: "That's my problem."

So what should the intro show? Not 400 views. Show the graph going down. Show the actual thing that made them click.

This is what I mean by "Show, Don't Tell."

When a creator mentions something, don't just put words on screen. Show the actual thing:

- Creator talks about a topic rising in popularity? Pull up the Google Trends graph.
- Creator mentions a salary from a job listing? Show the actual LinkedIn article.
- Creator references another creator? Show their real profile picture, not a generic avatar.

Here's another example. One of my students was editing a video where the creator talked about his following growing. The previous editor just had numbers going up on screen. Random text animation.

My student showed the creator's actual Twitter profile with the follower count increasing. Real account. Real numbers.

That's the difference. Generic placeholders vs. the actual thing.

The viewer made a decision to click based on a specific promise. Your job is to immediately confirm: yes, you're in the right place. This video is going to solve that exact problem.

## Chapter 5: Creative Director, Not an Editor

A client I worked with for over two years recently reached out to me.

Not for editing. For thumbnails.

I use Canva. I genuinely suck at Photoshop. There are hundreds of designers more technically skilled than me.

But they didn't reach out to those people. They reached out to me.

Why?

Because after two years, I understood their channel better than almost anyone. I understood their audience. I understood what they were trying to accomplish.

I had the creative director skill set. I knew the "what." The "how" of making a thumbnail in Canva was the easy part.

That's when I realized: I wasn't an editor anymore.

When you make this shift, you stop being defined by your technical skills. You stop competing with every other editor who knows Premiere and After Effects.

You become the person who just gets it. The person they trust with their content. The person they reach out to for things that aren't even in your job description.

## Outro

Stop asking "how do I make this look good?"

Start asking "what does the viewer need to see?"

**What, Not How.** Every decision starts with what the viewer needs.

**The Blockout Graphic.** One visual system that guides the viewer. Keeps them oriented. Never confused.

**Show, Don't Tell.** Show the actual thing that made them click. Deliver on the packaging.

I went from $1K a month after two years of grinding to $8K a month in half a year. Not because I became a better editor. Because I stopped being one.

If you want to go deeper on these frameworks, I built a free community where I break this stuff down. Link in the description.

But the shift starts with one question: are you editing to impress other editors, or are you editing to serve the viewer?

Answer that honestly, and you'll know exactly where to focus next.
