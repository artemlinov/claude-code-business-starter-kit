# WHY I QUIT EDITING

## Hook

Why does getting better at editing not actually make you more money?

For two years, I did everything you're supposed to do. Pirated all the plugins. Mastered After Effects. Watched every tutorial...

And you know what happened? My income barely moved. I was scraping by, making $1K on a good month, but then stressing out because I didn't know where the next video would come from.

Then I stopped focusing on editing. And within half a year, I was making $8K a month from one client.

Same technical skills. Same software. I didn't suddenly become a better editor overnight.

What changed was how I thought about my role entirely.

See, most editors are stuck in what I call "button-pusher" mode. They wait for instructions, execute them, send the video back. That's it. And there's a ceiling on what button-pushers can charge.

The editors making real money have made a shift. They stopped pushing buttons like editors and started thinking like creative directors.

In this video, I'm going to show you exactly what that shift looks like. I'll break down the three frameworks that completely changed how I approach editing, and show you why the creative director shift took me from barely scraping by to having my dream clients begging to work with me.

## Chapter 1: The Producer Who Couldn't Edit

When I started working with my main client, there was this producer named Marco.

Marco was not a good editor. Technically, he was kind of bad actually. His After Effects skills were nothing special. His cuts weren't perfectly timed. He wasn't doing anything flashy.

But the videos he produced were bangers.

That was the first moment I realized: okay, there's something more going on here.

I started paying attention to what Marco was actually doing. And one thing stood out immediately.

He would leave static graphics on screen for 5, sometimes 10 seconds. No animation. No movement. Just... sitting there.

In a normal editor world, that's a big no-no. You want things moving. You want stuff popping up. You want the video to feel dynamic.

But Marco understood something most editors don't: those static graphics were exactly what the viewer wanted to look at. They explained what the viewer wanted to learn out of the video. And the viewer needed TIME to actually process them.

He wasn't thinking about how to make things look cool. He was thinking about what the viewer needed to see.

Meanwhile, the client was trialing other editors. And they kept failing.

These were technically skilled editors. Good portfolios. Knew their way around Premiere and After Effects.

But they wouldn't listen to Marco. They kept forcing their own style onto the videos. They'd add animations for phrases that were completely redundant. Phrases the viewer wasn't even interested in.

They should have removed those phrases entirely. Instead, they added their flair on top to make it look cool.

The truth is: the viewer doesn't care about that stuff.

That's when I started to understand the difference between an editor and a creative director.

## Chapter 2: What, Not How

Here's the framework Marco taught me, even if he never said it out loud.

Most editors ask: HOW do I make this look good?

How do I animate this graphic? How do I make this transition smoother? How do I add more visual interest?

Creative directors ask a different question: WHAT should the viewer see right now?

What does the viewer need to understand at this moment? What are they expecting after clicking on this title and thumbnail? What would make them feel like this video is delivering on its promise?

The "what" comes first. The "how" is just execution.

And here's the thing most editors miss: sometimes the answer to "what should the viewer see" is... nothing. Remove it. That phrase is redundant. That animation is only making the video more confusing.

I'll be honest: when I was a button-pusher, the number one fear I had was not having animations for a while during the video.

If there was a 30-second stretch with no animation, I would panic. 45 seconds? I'd be scrambling to add something. A full minute? No way. The client is definitely going to send this back with revisions.

That fear is what keeps editors stuck. You're so scared the client won't accept the video that you fill every second with movement. But you're solving the wrong problem.

The client doesn't care if there's animation every 10 seconds. The client cares if the video works. If viewers understand the content. If the video delivers on its promise.

When I made the shift to "what, not how," that fear disappeared. Because now I had a reason for every decision. If there were no animations for 45 seconds, it was because just listening to what's being said was exactly what the viewer wanted.

The editors who kept failing with my client couldn't understand this. They saw every moment as an opportunity to add something. To show off their skills. To make things move.

But adding things isn't creative direction. Knowing what to add AND what to remove... that's creative direction.

When I made this shift, everything changed. I stopped asking "how do I make this intro flashy?" and started asking "the viewer just clicked on this title and thumbnail. That's all they know. What do they expect to see next?"

It sounds simple. But it changes everything about how you edit.

Now, you might be thinking: "Okay, but all this thinking on top of executing... that's going to take way more time. I already spend a week on each video. If I have to think about all this creative direction stuff too, I'll never finish anything."

Here's what actually happens.

When you're a button-pusher, you spend hours creating graphics and animations that should have never been created in the first place. You add flair to phrases that are redundant. You create things that don't serve the viewer.

Then you get revisions. And you redo half of it.

When you think before you execute, you're only creating things that need to exist. You're not wasting hours perfecting redundant animations. You're not adding graphics that confuse the viewer.

The result? You're faster. And the video is better.

## Chapter 3: The Blockout Graphic

Let me show you the technique that made my career.

I call it the Blockout Graphic. It's one graphic that you show multiple times throughout the video, guiding the viewer by revealing or changing elements as the video progresses.

It's not flashy. It doesn't look impressive in a portfolio. If you showed it to other editors, they'd probably think it's boring.

But here's what it does: it tells a story.

Because story equals change. When you pop up the same graphic multiple times with one element changing, you're literally telling a story visually. The viewer sees the progression. They feel like they're learning something.

And more importantly, the Blockout Graphic keeps the viewer grounded.

Think about what happens in most videos. The creator explains something, then goes off on a tangent, shows some B-roll, gives an example... and by the time they come back to the main point, the viewer has forgotten the main point.

The Blockout Graphic solves this. Every time you pop it up, you're reminding the viewer: here's where we are. Here's what we're learning. Here's how this example connects to the bigger picture.

Most editors make a new graphic for every point. Creative directors make ONE system that appears multiple times. The graphic itself becomes the structure of the video.

This is what I mean by digestibility over flashiness. A "boring" graphic that helps the viewer understand beats a flashy graphic that confuses them every single time.

## Chapter 4: Show, Don't Tell

Here's another framework that separates editors from creative directors.

When a creator says something, most editors think: how do I visualize this?

Creative directors think: what is the ACTUAL thing they're talking about? And can I show THAT instead of a generic placeholder?

Let me give you a real example.

One of the editors in my community was working on a video about what to do if your YouTube video is dying after 24 hours.

In the intro, he showed a view count: 400 views.

Seems reasonable, right? The video is about dying videos. 400 views looks like a dying video.

But here's the problem.

The people watching this video aren't all small creators. Some of them have decent-sized channels. For them, even a dying video gets way more than 400 views.

When they see 400 views on screen, they immediately think: "This video isn't for me. My videos get more than that even when they flop."

You just lost a chunk of your audience in the first 10 seconds.

Now look at the thumbnail. It shows a YouTube analytics graph plummeting after 24 hours. That downward curve.

That's what the viewer clicked for. They saw that graph going down and thought: "That's my problem. I need to fix that."

So what should the editor show in the intro? Not 400 views. Show the graph going down.

Show the actual thing that made them click.

This is what I mean by delivering on the packaging. The viewer made a decision to click based on a specific promise. Your job as the editor is to immediately confirm: yes, you're in the right place. This video is going to solve that exact problem.

Generic placeholders break that connection. The actual thing reinforces it.

## Chapter 5: Beyond Editing

Recently, a client I worked with for over two years reached out to me.

Not for editing. For thumbnails.

Now, I'm not a good thumbnail designer. I use Canva. I suck at Photoshop.

There are hundreds of designers more technically skilled than me at making thumbnails.

But they didn't reach out to those people. They reached out to me.

Why?

Because after two years, I understood their channel better than almost anyone. I understood their audience. I understood what they were trying to accomplish with their content.

I had the creative director skill set. I knew the "what." The "how" of making a thumbnail in Canva was the easy part.

And that's when I realized: I wasn't an editor anymore.

When you make this shift, you stop being defined by your technical skills. You stop competing with every other editor who knows Premiere and After Effects.

You become the person who just gets it. The person they trust with their content. The person they reach out to for things that aren't even in your job description.

That's what happens when you stop thinking like an editor and start thinking like a creative director.

## Outro

So here's the shift.

Stop asking "how do I make this look good?" Start asking "what does the viewer need to see?"

The three frameworks:

**What, Not How.** Every decision starts with what the viewer needs, not how to make it flashy.

**The Blockout Graphic.** One visual system that guides the viewer throughout the video. Digestibility, keeping the viewer grounded, making the viewer valuable and easy-to-understand beats flashiness every time.

**Show, Don't Tell.** Show the actual thing that made them click. Deliver on the packaging. Instead of spending hours visualizing things that are being said, go and look up that article online, that tweet or another youtube video that the creator is referencing.

I went from $1K a month after two years of grinding to $8K a month in the next half year. Not because I became a better editor. Because I stopped being an editor entirely.

But the shift starts with one question: are you editing to impress other editors, or are you editing to serve the viewer?

Answer that honestly, and you'll know exactly where to focus next.
