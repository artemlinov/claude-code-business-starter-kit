# WHY I QUIT EDITING

## Hook

Why does getting better at editing not actually make you more money?

For two years, I did everything you're supposed to do. Pirated all the plugins. Mastered After Effects. Watched every tutorial...

And you know what happened? My income barely moved. I was scraping by, making $1K on a good month, stressing about where the next video would come from.

Then I stopped focusing on editing. Half a year later: $8K a month from one client.

Same software. Same technical skills. What changed was how I thought about my role entirely.

Most editors are button-pushers. They wait for instructions, execute, send the video back. And there's a ceiling on what button-pushers can charge.

The editors making real money? They've made a shift that most editors don't even know exists.

---

## Chapter 1: The Button-Pusher

I met Marco in July, 2023. And he was not a good editor.

Technically, he was kind of bad. His After Effects skills were nothing special. He wasn't doing anything flashy.

But the videos he produced? You'd watch them in one sitting. Couldn't look away. You wouldn't even notice the editing because you were so sucked into the story.

Marco was a producer managing me and a few other editors. And I was excited to work with this client. I wanted to over-deliver. I wanted to prove that I was worth hiring.

So I did what any editor would do. I added animations. I made things move. I made things pop up. I filled every second with something visual because that's what I thought "good editing" meant.

But here's the thing that confused me. The feedback I'd get back from Marco? It was never about the animations. He never said "nice keyframes" or "that transition was smooth." His feedback was about completely different things.

And I couldn't figure out if my work was even good. Because I was evaluating my own editing by asking: are there enough animations? Do they look flashy enough?

Marco was evaluating the videos by asking something entirely different. And at the time, I didn't even know what that question was.

All I knew was that the first few projects were incredibly challenging. Not because the editing was hard. Because I didn't understand what made the editing good.

---

## Chapter 2: What, Not How

A few weeks in, I was on a call with Marco reviewing a cut. And there was this one section that had been bugging me. No animations for about a minute. Just the creator talking.

I brought it up. "We don't have any animations here for a while. Should I add something?"

Marco didn't even hesitate. "Nah, that's totally fine. He's just explaining the concept. The viewer is interested in what he's saying. It doesn't need anything."

That was the first time something clicked. Marco wasn't thinking about the edit at all. He was thinking about the viewer.

And once I started paying attention to how he made decisions, I saw it everywhere.

Most editors ask: HOW do I make this look good? How do I animate this? How do I make this transition smoother?

Marco was asking: WHAT should the viewer see right now? What do they need to understand? What would make them feel like this video is delivering on its promise?

The "what" comes first. The "how" is just execution.

And the thing is, Marco tried teaching this to other editors. They wouldn't listen.

They kept forcing their own style onto the videos. They'd add animations for phrases that the viewer didn't even care about. The phrases that they should have removed entirely. Instead, they added flashy effects on top to make it look cool.

The truth is: the viewer doesn't care about that stuff.

Adding things isn't creative direction. Knowing what to add AND what to remove... that's creative direction.

When I made this shift, everything changed. I stopped asking "how do I make this intro flashy?" and started asking "the viewer just clicked on this title and thumbnail. That's all they know. What do they expect to see next?"

It sounds simple. But it changes everything about how you edit.

## Chapter 3: When Less Is More

Marco would do this thing that used to drive me crazy.

He would leave a static, a bit ugly graphic on screen for 5, 10, sometimes 15 seconds. No animation. No movement. Just sitting there.

In a normal editor world, that's a big no-no. You want things moving. You want stuff popping up. You want the video to feel dynamic.

But those graphics showed the viewer exactly what they were interested in. And the viewer needed TIME to actually process them.

If Marco had added crazy keyframes and flashy animations to those graphics, the video would have been worse. The viewer would have gotten distracted by the editing instead of actually absorbing the information.

My favorite quote ever is from Steve Jobs. He said "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication."

If you imagine the midwit curve meme, where the guy on the left and the guy on the right end up doing the same thing, while the guy in the middle is trying too hard.

That's exactly what's happening with editors. They try too hard to show off their skills so that the client feels like the video is worth their money.

But more keyframes and animations doesn't mean a better video.

I can't tell you how many times I've created a complex animation, then intentionally removed elements to make it simpler. Because the viewer doesn't need to see my After Effects skills. They need to understand the point.

Knowing what to add AND what to remove... that's the creative director skill set you should be after.

## Chapter 4: What Simple Looks Like

Let me show you what simple animations actually look like in my editing.

Every single video I edited in the last 3 years has this one type of graphic no one talks about.

I call it the Blockout Graphic.

It's one graphic that you show multiple times throughout the video. You reveal or change elements as the video progresses. The viewer sees the same structure again and again, but each time something is different.

It doesn't look impressive in a portfolio. Other editors would probably think it's boring.

But here's why it works.

Most editors think the number one thing that makes viewers click off is boredom. That's why they're scared to show still graphics. That's why they add movement to everything.

Wrong.

The number one thing that makes viewers click off is confusion.

Think about a 15-minute video. That's a lot of information. If you don't structure it, it feels like one long sentence. The viewer loses track of where they are. They don't know what section they're in. They don't know how the example connects to the main point.

They get confused. They leave.

The Blockout Graphic solves this.

I break every video into what I call Story Blocks. Distinct sections with clear purposes. And within each Story Block, I use a Blockout Graphic to guide the viewer through.

Every time I pop up the same graphic with one element changed, I'm telling a visual story. The viewer sees progression. They know exactly where they are. They never get lost.

Here's a real example.

I was editing an interview where the founder talked about building two different apps. The host asked: "How did you come up with the idea for both of these?"

Without the Blockout Graphic, his answer would feel random. Just a guy talking about two separate things.

With the Blockout Graphic, I structured both explanations the same way. Same graphic format. Same progression. Suddenly his answer felt like a repeatable blueprint. Something the viewer could actually copy.

That's what makes this graphic powerful. It doesn't just display information. It creates structure. It turns random answers into frameworks.

And here's the key: this graphic requires thinking through the entire section BEFORE I touch After Effects. I'm planning the visual story in my V0 cut. Most editors jump straight to making graphics. That's executing. Creative directors plan first. That's thinking.

Here are the other simple graphics I use constantly:

- Creator talks about a topic rising in popularity? Pull up the Google Trends graph.
- Creator mentions a salary from a job listing? Show the actual LinkedIn article.
- Creator references another creator? Show their real profile picture, not a generic avatar.

These aren't complex animations. They're the actual thing. Simple, but effective.

Notice a pattern? None of these graphics are impressive. They're all simple. But they work because I thought about WHAT to show, not HOW to make it flashy.

## Chapter 5: Think More, Execute Less

Here's what actually changed when I went from $1K to $8K a month.

I didn't get better at After Effects. I didn't learn new plugins. I didn't get faster at executing.

I started spending way more time thinking. And way less time executing.

Let me break this down:

Decide WHAT before HOW. That's thinking.

Decide IF you need the animation before adding it. That's thinking.

When you do add, keep it simple. That's thinking.

Plan your blockout graphic before making it. That's thinking.

Find the actual thing instead of creating a placeholder. That's thinking.

Now you might be thinking: all this thinking is going to take more time.

Here's what actually happens.

Button-pushers spend hours creating graphics that end up getting cut. They add animations that should never exist. They build complexity that confuses viewers. Then they get revisions. And redo half of it.

Creative directors think before they execute. They only create what needs to exist. They're not perfecting redundant animations. They're not redesigning graphics that didn't work.

The result? You're faster. And the video is better.

That's the shift. Creative Director doesn't mean "better executor." It means "better thinker."

Stop being a button-pusher. Stop filling time with execution. Start being a creative director. Start filling time with intention.

I went from $1K a month to $8K a month not by executing better, but by thinking better.
