# WHY I QUIT EDITING

## Hook

Why does getting better at editing not actually make you more money?

For two years, I did everything you're supposed to do. Pirated all the plugins. Mastered After Effects. Watched every tutorial…

And you know what happened? My income barely moved. I was scraping by, making $1k on a good month, but then stressing out because I didn’t know where the next video would come from.

Then I stopped focusing on editing. And within half a year, I was making $8K a month from one client.

Same technical skills. Same software. I didn't suddenly become a better editor overnight.

What changed was how I thought about my role entirely.

Most editors are button-pushers. They wait for instructions, execute them, and send the video back. That's it. And there's a ceiling on what button-pushers can charge.

The editors making real money? They're not better at pressing buttons. They've made a shift that most editors don't even know exists.

In this video, I'm going to break down exactly what that shift is, why it took me two years of struggling to figure it out, and how you can make it way faster than I did.

## Chapter 1: The Button-Pusher Problem

Let me show you what I mean.

I recently hired an editor to help with a vlog. Gave him the footage, gave him my vision for the video, gave him a decent amount of instructions.

He delivered on time. Technically, the edit was fine. Cuts were clean. Music was okay. Nothing was broken.

But the video was unwatchable.

It was robotic. Boring. You could tell it wasn't made for a viewer. It was made to check boxes.

He didn't think about the packaging. Didn't think about what I was trying to accomplish with this video. Didn't think about who would actually watch this and why they'd care.

He just... edited.

And here's the thing: he probably followed my instructions. That's the problem. He followed them without understanding them. Without asking himself: what is this video actually trying to do?

I ended up having to redo most of it myself. Not because his technical skills were bad. But because he was editing like an editor.

He was focused on the how. How do I make these cuts smooth? How do I sync this to music? How do I make this transition look clean?

He never asked the what. What is the viewer supposed to feel here? What are they expecting after clicking this title? What does this moment need to land?

That's the difference between an editor and a creative director.

Editors execute. Creative directors think.

## Chapter 3: The Producer Who Couldn't Edit

When I first started working with my main client, there was this producer named Marco.

Marco was not a good editor. Technically, he was kind of bad actually. His After Effects skills were nothing special. His cuts weren't perfectly timed. He wasn't doing anything flashy.

But the videos he produced were bangers.

That was the first moment I realized: okay, there's something more going on here.

Marco wasn't thinking about how to make things look cool. He was thinking about what the viewer needed to see. He was thinking about the story. About the promise the title made and how every single second of the video either delivered on that promise or didn't.

I started paying attention to what he was actually doing. And I realized the technical editing was almost irrelevant. What mattered was the thinking behind every decision.

That's when the shift started for me. Slowly. Gradually.

I stopped asking "how do I make this intro look as flashy as possible?"

I started asking "the viewer just clicked on this title and thumbnail. That's all they know. What do they expect to see next? How do I deliver that?"

It sounds simple. But it changes everything about how you edit.

## Chapter 4: What, Not How

Let me give you a concrete example.

Yesterday I did a masterclass in my community breaking down a specific type of graphic I use all the time.

This graphic is not flashy at all. No crazy animations. No 3D. Nothing that would make an editor's portfolio pop.

If I was a button-pusher, I'd be thinking: how do I make this graphic look better? What effects can I add? How do I make this more impressive?

But that's the wrong question.

The right question is: what is this graphic showing? What does the viewer need to understand at this moment? How do I make that as clear as possible?

All of my effort goes into the what. Not the how.

Because viewers don't care how cool your graphics look. They care whether they understood the point. They care whether the video delivered on the promise of the title.

This is what I mean by thinking like a creative director instead of an editor.

An editor asks: how do I execute this?

A creative director asks: what does this need to accomplish?

The execution part is easy once you know what you're trying to do. Most editors have the skills to execute anything. What they're missing is the thinking that tells them what to execute in the first place.

## Chapter 5: The Cheat Code

Here's something most editors get completely wrong: revisions.

Every time you get a revision, you have two options.

Option one: make the change, send it back, move on. This is what most editors do. They treat revisions like an annoyance. Something to get through as fast as possible.

Option two: ask yourself why the client wanted this change.

Think of it like this. You're in a math exam. You haven't prepared at all. But your friend slips you a cheat sheet. Not just the answers, but the full solutions.

Most editors copy the final answer and throw away the sheet. They never ask why. They just make the change and forget about it.

The editors who level up? They study the solutions. They find the patterns. They ask: what does this revision tell me about how this client thinks? What do they actually care about? What are they optimizing for that I'm not seeing?

Eventually, you don't need the cheat sheet anymore. You start thinking like them.

Every revision is a free lesson in how your client's brain works. If you're paying attention.

## Chapter 6: Zero Revisions

About six months into working with my main client, something happened that changed everything.

I submitted a V1. And it came back with zero revisions.

Not "one small note." Zero. Just "approved."

At that point, they had been trying out so many other editors. And none of them were working out. These were technically skilled editors. Good portfolios. Knew their way around Premiere and After Effects.

But they kept forcing their own style onto the videos. They kept trying to make things look cool from their perspective, not from the viewer's perspective. They were editing to impress other editors, not to serve the audience.

When I got that zero revision video, I realized: I'd crossed over. I wasn't just editing anymore. I was thinking like the person paying me.

That's when I raised my rate from around $1K to $2K per video.

Not because I got better at editing. But because I'd become something more valuable than an editor.

## Chapter 7: Depth Over Volume

Here's where this gets interesting.

After two years of working with this client, we eventually stopped working together. I wanted to focus on building my own thing.

A few months go by. No contact.

Then out of nowhere, they reach out. Not for editing. For thumbnails.

Now, I'm not the best thumbnail designer. There are people way more skilled than me at that specific thing.

But they didn't reach out to those people. They reached out to me.

Why?

Because after two years, I understood their channel better than almost anyone. I understood their audience. I understood what they were trying to accomplish with their content. I had context that no one else had.

That's the power of depth over volume.

Most editors are trying to get more clients. More projects. More videos. They think the path to making more money is doing more work.

But there's a ceiling on that. You can only edit so many videos. And when your entire business model is "edit more," you have zero leverage. You can be replaced by two editors who are slightly slower than you.

The editors charging premium rates aren't playing that game. They're going deep with fewer clients. Building so much context and understanding that they become irreplaceable.

When you become the person who just gets it, opportunities come to you that have nothing to do with your job title. You're not just an editor anymore. You're the person they trust with their content.

---

## Chapter 8: The Shift

So what's the actual shift here?

It's not about learning new skills. You probably already have the technical skills you need.

It's about changing what you optimize for.

Button-pushers optimize for the edit. How clean are my cuts? How smooth are my transitions? How impressive do my effects look?

Creative directors optimize for the viewer. What does the viewer need to feel right now? What did the title promise them? Am I delivering on that promise or am I just showing off?

When you make this shift, everything changes.

You stop getting endless revisions because you're already thinking like the client.

You stop competing on price because you're not a commodity anymore.

You stop chasing more clients because the clients you have keep expanding what they trust you with.

I went from $1K a month after two years of grinding to $8K a month in the next eight months. Then $10K months. Not because I became a better editor. Because I stopped being an editor entirely.

The title of "video editor" is the ceiling. The moment you let go of it, there's no limit to where you can go.

Answer that honestly, and you'll know exactly where to focus next.
