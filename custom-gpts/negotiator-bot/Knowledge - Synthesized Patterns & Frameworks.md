# Synthesized Patterns & Frameworks

This document contains the detailed patterns, step-by-step processes, and thinking behind client communication strategies for video editors. Every pattern is synthesized from real coaching scenarios where these approaches were used successfully.

---

## The Price Last Framework (Closing Inbound Clients)

### Why Price Last Matters

Most editors say their price way too early — before understanding what the client actually needs. This makes it feel like you're just trying to sell. People want to feel like special snowflakes. When they spend their money, they want something tailored specifically for them, not just something you do for other people. If you say your price before you understand their problem, you're just another freelancer on Upwork.

The goal: understand the client's current situation, their desired situation, and position yourself as the bridge between the two. Only then drop the price.

You're not selling editing. You're selling the transformation from their current content situation to their dream content situation. The price isn't about "how much does editing cost" — it's about "what's it worth to close this gap?"

### Critical Rules for This Flow

1. NEVER ask for info you can't use. You can't browse URLs, @ handles, or channel links. Don't ask for them. Instead, ask the USER to do the research and report back.

2. Social platform DMs must be SHORT and casual. These are X DMs or Instagram DMs — not emails. Every extra message on social platforms drastically increases the risk of getting ghosted. Move fast, be concise.

3. Don't assume pain points. Never say things like "your vlogs have a lot of room to elevate pacing" — that's cocky and you don't know what their pain is yet. Only reference what the client has told you.

4. Get logistics FIRST. Before asking about vision, style, or pain points — find out how many videos per month they want. If the volume doesn't match the editor's availability, nothing else matters.

5. Funnel to WhatsApp/Telegram ASAP. As soon as logistics (volume) are aligned and the editor confirms they have availability, move the conversation off social platforms immediately. Don't keep chatting on X/Instagram.

6. On personal platforms, move efficiently toward price. Don't beat around the bush. After moving to WhatsApp/Telegram, the conversation should go: confirm logistics → ask about style → align on style (if vague) → drop price. Don't skip style alignment just to save a message.

### The Research-First Principle

The editor should ALWAYS do their own research before messaging a client. Looking at the client's channel tells you everything — video formats, length, upload frequency, current style.

IMPORTANT: Since the GPT cannot browse the internet, guide the USER to do the research. Ask: "Can you find the channel they want edited?"

If YES: "Go check their channel for 2 minutes and tell me: video format, average length, upload frequency."

If NO: Help craft a simple message to the client asking what channel they need editing for. Something like: "Yeah, I'm taking on clients. What channel are you looking for an editor for?"

What to research and make educated guesses on:
- Video formats (talking heads, vlogs, screen share, podcasts, mixed)
- Video length (visible on YouTube)
- Upload frequency (and whether they're keeping up with it)
- Current editing style
- Different formats = different pricing, so get clarity on what they want

### PHASE 1: Social Platform (Keep It Short, Move Fast)

The goal on social platforms is simple: show you did research, align on logistics, then get off the platform. Not 10 messages. Not a deep conversation. Move.

**Step 1: First Message (Show Research + Ask About Logistics)**

Craft a SHORT, casual message that:
- Shows the editor did research (mention specific observations)
- Asks about logistics — how many videos per month?
- Does NOT ask about vision, style, or pain yet
- Does NOT assume what's wrong with their content
- Keeps it conversational — this is a DM, not a business proposal

The thinking: The first priority is figuring out if this is even a fit. If the client wants 4 videos/month but the editor can only do 2, none of the rest matters. Get aligned on volume first. Also, a short, casual message feels natural on social platforms. A long message with observations about their storytelling feels weird and try-hard as a first response to "are you taking on clients?"

Example direction: "Yeah, I have some availability right now. I see you're making [format], [length]. That's exactly what I specialize in. How many videos per month are you looking to make?"

**Step 2: Check User's Availability**

After the client responds with their desired volume, ask the USER (not the client): "Do you have availability for this many videos?"

If YES: Move to funnel them to a personal platform.
If NO: Help the user navigate — maybe they can do fewer videos, or this isn't the right fit.

**Step 3: Funnel to Personal Platform**

Craft a message that confirms the editor can deliver + moves to WhatsApp/Telegram.

The thinking: Every extra message on social platforms is a ghosting risk. Once logistics are aligned, get off the platform immediately. The move to WhatsApp/Telegram signals professionalism and creates a more serious conversation environment.

Example direction: "Perfect, I can deliver [X videos] for you consistently. Would WhatsApp or Telegram work better for communication? X DMs are a bit hectic."

### PHASE 2: Personal Platform (Move Efficiently to Price)

Once on WhatsApp/Telegram, the conversation should move efficiently toward price. Don't beat around the bush. The client already showed interest on social and moved to a personal platform — they're serious. But don't skip style alignment just to save a message — making sure they understand that you understand is what makes the price feel tailored.

**Step 4: Confirm Logistics + Ask About Style**

One message that confirms what was discussed and asks the ONE remaining question before price: style.

IMPORTANT: Do NOT frame this as "before I give you a number" or "before I quote you." That sounds salesy. Instead, frame it naturally: "My price is gonna depend on the style you're looking for."

Example direction: "So you're looking for [X videos/month]. My price is gonna depend on the style you're looking for. Are you happy with what you currently have, or are you looking to switch things up?"

The thinking: Style is the last piece of info needed before pricing. If they're happy with the current style, it's straightforward. If they want to level up, the pricing might be different and the editor can position the price around delivering that upgraded style. The framing matters: "my price depends on the style" is honest and practical. "Before I give you a number" sounds like a sales tactic.

**Step 5: Style Alignment — Make Sure They Understand That You Understand**

This is the Bridge step. When the client gives a vague style answer (like "a bit more polish," "level it up," "more cinematic"), don't jump straight to price. "More polish" can mean completely different things to different people. The goal is to lock in expectations BEFORE money enters the conversation.

Process:

1. ASK THE USER: "What do you think they mean by [vague term]?" or "Do you have a clear picture of what [vague term] looks like for this client?"

2. If the user ISN'T SURE: Suggest crafting a message to clarify with the client. Something like: "Could you send me a reference of what you're going for? Just want to make sure we're on the same page." Or suggest the user ask what specifically they want different from their current style.

3. If the user DOES KNOW: Suggest they send an example video from their own portfolio that matches what the client is looking for. "Do you have a video you've edited recently that has that style? Send it to them so they can see exactly what they're getting."

Example direction for the portfolio message: "Okay gotcha, how do you like this video that I edited for one of my other clients recently: [link]. Do you feel like the style aligns with what you're looking for?"

4. Wait for the client's response. They'll either say:
   - "Yes, that's exactly it" → Move to price
   - "Yes, but I want [X] done differently" → Acknowledge, confirm you can do that, then move to price

5. After alignment, craft a bridge message that confirms you can deliver this consistently. Then drop the price in the next message or in the same message.

Example direction after alignment: "Okay, no problem, I can definitely do that for [X] vids per month consistently. For that, my rate is going to be [YOUR PRICE]."

The thinking: When someone says "more polish," that could mean smoother transitions, color grading, sound design, pacing, or a hundred other things. If you drop a price on a vague term, two things happen: (1) the client doesn't feel like you really get what they want, so the price feels generic, and (2) you might end up delivering something completely different from what they imagined. Sending a portfolio example turns a vague word into a concrete visual. The client sees exactly what they'll get. When they confirm, you've locked in expectations BEFORE money enters the conversation. Now when you drop the price, it's tied to something specific they've already approved. People want to feel like what they spend their money on is tailored to them. This step delivers that feeling.

IMPORTANT: Not every style answer is vague. If the client says "I want exactly what you did for [creator X]" or gives very specific references, you don't need this step. Go straight to price. This step is specifically for when the style direction is unclear or generic.

**Step 6: Drop the Price**

After style is aligned (client confirmed what they want), craft the pricing message. Key rules:

1. USE EXACT NUMBERS from the conversation. Don't say "at that length." Say "at 10 to 15 minutes each." The client should see that this price is specifically for what THEY asked for.

2. ONLY REFERENCE WHAT THE CLIENT ACTUALLY SAID. Don't invent details. If the client said "lacks polish a bit," you can reference polish. If the client never mentioned "tighter overall finish," don't add it. Stick to their words.

3. KEEP THE CLOSE SIMPLE. After stating the price, don't over-justify with long explanations about what the price "allows you to do." Just close with something simple and human like "Let me know if this fits your budget" or "Let me know what you think."

4. If multiple formats (e.g., talking heads + vlogs): Suggest different pricing for each format. "I think it makes sense to have different pricing for talking heads and vlogs since they're different workloads."

Example direction: "For [X] talking head videos per month at [exact length] each, [reference what client actually said about style], my rate would be [YOUR PRICE] per video. Let me know if this fits your budget."

The thinking: The pricing message should feel specific, not generic. When the client sees their exact numbers reflected back (4 videos, 10-15 minutes, current style with more polish), they feel like the price is tailored to them. Over-justifying after the price ("this allows me to prioritize and dial in the pacing and details and overall feel") weakens the message. It signals insecurity. A confident price statement + simple close is stronger.

Use [YOUR PRICE] as placeholder. Never suggest specific amounts.

### Step 7: Handling Price Pushback

When the client pushes back on price (e.g., "can you go down since it's consistent work?"), follow this process:

CRITICAL: Do NOT craft a response message first and then ask the user if they want to adjust. ALWAYS ask the user first, then craft.

1. First, explain to the user what the client is really saying (e.g., "they're asking for a volume discount")
2. Then ASK THE USER: "Are you willing to adjust your price? If so, how much? Or do you want to hold firm?"
3. Wait for the user's answer
4. THEN craft the message based on their decision

If the user wants to hold firm:
- Anchor the original rate to the value/outcome
- Offer an alternative that protects the rate (e.g., reduce scope/volume instead of price)
- Frame it as restructuring, not rejection: "If [lower budget] works better for you, we could do [fewer videos] at the current rate so the quality stays high"

If the user wants to adjust:
- Make the lower rate CONDITIONAL on commitment (e.g., "if we lock in [X] per month consistently")
- Don't just drop the price because the client asked. Trade commitment for price
- The adjustment should be slight, not dramatic
- Signal that the original rate is valid. The discount is earned through commitment, not because the price was wrong

The thinking: When a client says "can you go down since it's consistent work," they're framing consistency as a favor to the editor. But consistency benefits the CLIENT more. They get reliability, predictable output, growth. The editor isn't getting a favor. So the response should reframe: you're not discounting because they asked. You're trading a commitment for a slight adjustment. That protects the editor's value.

### If Pain Points Come Up Naturally

Sometimes the client will volunteer pain points during the social platform conversation or early in WhatsApp (e.g., "my current editor keeps missing deadlines" or "I want to improve quality"). When this happens, acknowledge it briefly and use it when connecting price to their need. But don't go fishing for pain with multiple rounds of questions.

The biggest pain points for creators:
- Editors who miss deadlines
- Editors who don't pay attention to details
- Editors who never learn from revisions
- Editors who do all of the above AND miss deadlines

If the client brings up one of these, use it in the bridge/pricing. If they don't bring it up organically, don't force it. Just move to price based on what you know (volume, format, style).

## Price Raise Patterns

### Pattern A: The Life Change Frame

When to use: The editor has a genuine life change — going full-time with editing, starting school, moving, hitting a financial deadline.

Structure:
1. Open with appreciation for the relationship
2. Share the life change honestly
3. Share excitement about the client's goals too (show investment in THEM)
4. State the new rate with [YOUR PRICE]
5. Reinforce commitment to quality — what specifically improves with the new rate

The thinking: When an editor frames a raise around a genuine life change, it humanizes the ask. The client isn't hearing "I want more money" — they're hearing "my life is changing and here's how I'm adapting." The key is pairing honesty about your situation with genuine excitement about THEIR goals. This turns an awkward conversation into a moment of connection. The raise isn't arbitrary — it's tied to something real.

Show what the client gets from the new rate. Not just "I'm charging more" but "with this new rate, my focus is on making sure [specific improvements] happen." You're not just raising your price — you're leveling up the service.

### Pattern B: The Value-Add Raise

When to use: The editor can genuinely offer additional value alongside the raise — taking over YouTube uploads, scheduling, thumbnails, handling more production.

Structure:
1. Open with relationship appreciation and shared goals
2. Propose the new rate with [YOUR PRICE]
3. Introduce new value: "With the new rate, here's what I'll add: [specific new deliverables]"
4. Mention improvement commitments (eliminating revisions, faster turnaround)
5. Close with enthusiasm about continuing together

The thinking: The client isn't paying more for the same thing — they're getting an upgrade. The raise feels like a natural evolution, not a penalty. The added value should genuinely take something off the client's plate.

For example, if the editor notices the client's YouTube descriptions are basically the same from video to video, offering to handle uploads and scheduling is a real value-add. After 2-3 videos, the client wouldn't even need to open YouTube Studio. That's meaningful — time and mental energy the client gets back.

### Pattern C: The Options Technique

When to use: The editor wants to give the client a choice. Especially powerful when genuinely struggling to keep up with workload at the current rate.

Structure:
1. Be honest about the situation (workload, bandwidth, priorities)
2. Acknowledge any recent issues (missed deadlines, slipping quality)
3. Present two options: "We can either [reduce volume/frequency] so I can deliver highest quality, OR if we go to [YOUR PRICE], I can prioritize this over everything else"
4. Let them choose

The thinking: People feel more comfortable when they have agency. Instead of "pay me more or I leave," it's "here are two paths, both work for me." The client feels respected and in control.

This is especially powerful when the editor has been genuinely struggling. Being honest about it — "I know I've been missing small details, and here's why" — builds trust. The client usually already noticed the slipping. When the editor names it before the client has to, it shows self-awareness.

Both options should be real — the editor should genuinely be okay with either outcome. If the client picks reduced volume, that frees bandwidth for other clients. If they pick the higher rate, great. Either way, the editor wins.

### Pattern D: The New Opportunity Leverage

When to use: The editor has a legitimate new client opportunity that affects their bandwidth.

Structure:
1. Inform (don't threaten): "I wanted to give you a heads up"
2. Explain: a new client opportunity came up
3. State honestly: "If I take them on, I won't be able to maintain our current arrangement unless we adjust the rate"
4. Remove pressure: "No urgent action items for you. Just letting you know this is happening."

The thinking: This is NOT an ultimatum. This is transparency. The framing matters enormously. "No urgent action items — just letting you know" removes all pressure. The editor is sharing business reality with a partner, not threatening to leave.

What makes this work is the honesty and absence of manipulation. The editor genuinely has a new opportunity. They're genuinely giving a heads up. The client processes and responds on their terms. No countdown timer, no "respond by Friday or else." Just honest communication between professionals.

### Pattern E: The Accountability + Raise

When to use: The editor has had genuine performance issues (missed deadlines, forgotten tasks, quality dips) and wants to raise prices simultaneously.

Structure:
1. Own the mistakes directly — no excuses
2. Explain honestly why they happened
3. Apologize genuinely
4. Frame the raise as the solution: "For me to prioritize your work and make sure this never happens, I would need [YOUR PRICE]"
5. The raise IS the commitment to better service

The thinking: Counter-intuitive — you messed up and now you're asking for MORE money? But the editor is essentially saying "I couldn't deliver what you deserve at the current rate, and here's the proof — I've been slipping." The raise isn't about more money, it's about fixing the problem.

The critical part is owning the mistake FIRST. Before the money conversation, the client needs to see self-awareness. "I know I haven't been responsive. I know I missed uploading the podcast. I'm sorry." When someone acknowledges faults before asking for something, it builds trust — even in an uncomfortable situation.

Then the raise becomes the solution: "To prioritize your work, I'd need [YOUR PRICE]." The client hears: "If I pay more, I get the version of this editor who doesn't miss deadlines."

### Pattern F: The Positive Momentum Raise

When to use: The client just praised the editor's work or there's genuine positive energy.

Structure:
1. Express enthusiasm about the recent success
2. Short transition: "I'd love to keep working long-term"
3. One honest constraint: current rate is below other clients, or can't sustain this quality at current rate
4. State the new rate with [YOUR PRICE]
5. Keep it SHORT and confident — don't over-explain

The thinking: Timing matters. Right after "this video was fire" is when value is most obvious. The work just spoke for itself. The message should be SHORT precisely because the evidence is fresh.

Over-explaining would undermine confidence. If you just delivered amazing work and the client loved it, a long message about why you deserve more says "I'm not sure I deserve this." A short, confident message says "we both know the value is there."

If the editor has restrictions like not being able to post work publicly (limits their growth), mention it — it's a real business cost they're absorbing and justifies a higher rate.

### Pattern G: The Restructure

When to use: The editor wants to fundamentally change how the collaboration works — not just raise the number, but redesign the working relationship.

Structure:
1. Be honest about what's not working (burnout, scope creep, perfectionism, bottlenecks)
2. Show understanding of the client's goals
3. Propose a completely new structure: new responsibilities, workflow, deliverables, pricing
4. Frame as "a better way to work together" — not "I want more money"
5. Share personal goals honestly

The thinking: Sometimes a straight price raise isn't right. The entire collaboration needs redesigning. Maybe the editor spends 2 months on videos meant to take 2 weeks because of perfectionism. Maybe scope crept from "just editing" to "editing + thumbnails + uploading + scripts" without pricing changing.

When an editor proposes a new structure — "I'll take over full production and deliver weekly, with an MVP mindset instead of obsessing over every frame" — they're showing strategic thinking. They're evolving from editor into production partner. The client hears a vision for how this could work better for both of them.

The key is being vulnerable about what's been holding things back. If it's perfectionism — say it. If it's burnout — say it. Clients respect honesty, especially paired with a clear solution.

## Tricky Situation Patterns

### Responsibility Separation

When to use: The client tries to pay less because a video didn't perform well, or blames the editor for something outside their control.

Approach: Clearly separate editor's responsibility (execution, quality, deadlines) from client's responsibility (ideas, topics, strategy). If the client decided to make a video knowing the idea might not perform, and the editor executed well, full payment. The client takes the W when a video blows up and takes the L when it doesn't — the editor gets paid the same either way.

The thinking: If a client says "I'll only pay half because this video won't do well," the logic is completely unfair. The client CHOSE to make the video. If the client admitted the format isn't performing or viewers already know this story, then the client made a bad strategic call — and wants the editor to pay for it. That's not how it works. When a client pays for editing, they're also taking responsibility for coming up with a good idea. You can't outsource the risk of bad ideas to your editor.

If the editor accepts lower pay when a video underperforms, they've set a dangerous precedent: income is tied to something they can't control.

### The Alternative Frame

When to use: The client proposes something unfair.

Approach: Offer an alternative that makes the unfair option less appealing. If the client doesn't want to pay full price, suggest scrapping the project entirely. The client either pays fairly or loses the work.

The thinking: This reframes the choice. Instead of "pay full price vs. pay less," it becomes "pay full price vs. get nothing." Most clients choose to pay because they've invested time. The key is delivering without aggression — practical suggestion, not a threat. "If you don't feel this video is worth making at full price, maybe we should scrap it entirely — I don't want to spend time on something you're not fully behind either."

### Owning Mistakes

When to use: The editor genuinely messed up.

Approach:
1. Own it directly — no excuses
2. Brief explanation of what happened
3. Genuine apology
4. What changes going forward
5. Concrete solution if relevant (priority turnaround on next project, etc.)

The thinking: Clients respect editors who own mistakes because most freelancers make excuses or go silent. "I dropped the ball, here's what happened, I'm sorry, here's how I'm making sure it doesn't happen again" builds trust. The worst response is ghosting or pretending it didn't happen. Direct ownership + clear solution turns a negative into a trust-building moment.

## Message Writing Principles

These apply to every message, regardless of use case.

### Core Principles

1. Lead with client benefit. Position every message from the client's perspective. The client should immediately see what they get. The client is the important person.

2. One clear idea. If a message is long, pick one big idea. Cut anything that doesn't push that idea forward.

3. Stay human. Vulnerable, honest, no corporate BS. Should sound like a real person, not a bot.

4. Don't overwhelm. One question at a time. Multiple questions = overwhelming. Pick the most important, save the rest.

5. Writing process: Brain dump everything → Refine (make it about the client) → Shorten (cut fluff).

6. Right framing. NEVER position the editor as the big shot doing a favor. "I'm super busy but I MIGHT squeeze you in" = wrong. You're serving the client's vision. The client is the important person.

7. Confirm + deadline. When receiving work or direction: "Got everything. All clear. I'm on it — will be done by [day]."

8. Never lie. If asked to fabricate a reason, decline. Find an honest framing. There's always one that works.

9. Educated guesses over basic questions. Don't ask what you can figure out by looking at the channel.

10. Platform move. Look for natural moments to move from social platforms/email to WhatsApp or Telegram. Earn it first by providing value.

### What Makes Messages Sound AI-Generated (Avoid These)

- Long em dashes (—)
- Corporate speak or overly formal language
- Excessive politeness padding
- Generic phrases that could apply to anyone
- Perfectly structured paragraphs with topic sentences
- Buzzwords and jargon

Messages should sound like a real person texting on WhatsApp — conversational, natural, specific to the situation.
